# Subjective Evaluation of Perceived Effects in AI-Assisted Design Workflows: A Systematic Literature Review

## 1 Introduction

Perceived effects refer to the effects of a system, feature, or intervention as experienced, interpreted, and judged by people. Subjective evaluation provides access to these perceived effects through people's accounts and judgments. This formulation synthesizes descriptive uses of perceived effects in research on explanations, collaboration, and interactive systems [cite:okoso2025] [cite:schleyer2012] [cite:ma2025]. In human-centered design, attention to people's needs, activities, and circumstances makes perceived effects relevant across different design domains [cite:iso2019]. For example, visual communication design may involve judgments about aesthetic appeal and the clarity of an intended message; industrial and product design may involve judgments about suitability, feasibility, and comfort; and fashion design may involve judgments about aesthetic expression, material qualities, and the anticipated experience of wearing a garment. Perceived effects also matter at different design stages. During research, subjective evaluation can establish the needs and value criteria against which perceived effects will subsequently be judged; during ideation, judgments about inspiration and novelty can direct exploration; during prototyping and testing, judgments about usability can guide revision; and during implementation, judgments about fit with everyday practices can inform continued adoption. Rigorous subjective evaluation of perceived effects is therefore fundamental for informing design decisions across the workflow and determining whether design outcomes meet the needs and expectations of the people they are intended to serve.

In human-computer interaction (HCI), perceived effects can encompass instrumental, affective, and situated aspects of interaction. Hassenzahl and Tractinsky's account of user experience emphasizes the interplay among the person, the system, and the circumstances of use [cite:hassenzahl2006]. In particular, AI-Assisted Design Workflows introduce perceived effects associated with AI's contribution to proposing, interpreting, and revising design material. A designer may experience generated suggestions as inspiring while also becoming concerned about fixation; perceive a reduction in effort while experiencing less control over a decision; or appreciate a finished outcome while questioning authorship and creative ownership. Research on AI-assisted ideation and human-AI co-creativity illustrates why the quality of a design outcome and the designer's experience require separate attention [cite:ahsan2026] [cite:cox2025]. The same intervention may also be judged differently by its operator, a domain expert, and an eventual user. Rigorous subjective evaluation of perceived effects in AI-Assisted Design Workflows consequently requires clear correspondence between the judgment being elicited, the person making it, the object of that judgment, and AI's role in the activity.

Credible evaluation evidence is central to this correspondence. The CDC Program Evaluation Framework connects an evaluation concept to an indicator and a measure, within a broader alignment of evaluation questions, study design, data sources, and expectations [cite:kidder2024]. Utilization-focused evaluation further emphasizes that evidence should serve identified purposes and intended users in a particular situation [cite:patton2008]. Drawing on these accounts, we examine three connected components of evidence for subjective evaluation: perceived effects, subjective indicators, and tools. We organize perceived effects into broader dimensions, preserve the indicators through which they are operationalized, and document the tools used to elicit the relevant judgments. We examine seven contextual factors: evaluation purpose (G1), design domain (G2), design stage (G3), evaluation object (G4), evaluator (G5), target user (G6), and AI function (G7). These are this review's analytical categories rather than an official taxonomy supplied by either evaluation framework. Selecting appropriate evidence requires understanding how its components relate to each other and to the context in which subjective evaluation is conducted.

Existing reviews provide substantial accounts of creativity support tools and evaluation in human-AI co-creativity [cite:remy2020] [cite:cox2025] [cite:ahsan2026]. Reviews of individual instruments also reveal the consequences of inconsistent implementation and reporting [cite:lee2026]. A complementary problem remains: how to connect the diverse perceived effects examined across AI-Assisted Design Workflows to their subjective indicators and tools, assess the reported support for those connections, and use contextual information to guide subsequent evaluation choices. A list of frequently used indicators cannot establish that they were appropriately measured. A list of named tools cannot establish that their interpretation was justified in a new population or task. Without examining both the evidence chain and its contextual dependencies, findings are difficult to compare, and researchers have limited grounds for selecting evidence for a different workflow.

In this work, we systematically review a corpus of 125 papers published from 2022 to the search cutoff of September 25, 2026. We first map the reported perceived effects, subjective indicators, and tools across publication years, design domains, and design stages (RQ1). We then conduct a structured assessment of reported evaluation evidence using seven standards adapted from the third edition of the JCSEE Program Evaluation Standards (RQ2) [cite:yarbrough2011]. We examine contextual associations and the relative predictive contribution of G1-G7 in strict and expanded analytical samples containing 63 and 183 records, respectively, defined by explicit operational eligibility rules (RQ3). This analysis includes sensitivity to how contextual categories were constructed. Finally, we translate the evidence and its limitations into guidelines and a progressive-disclosure recommender design for selecting perceived effects, subjective indicators, and tools (RQ4). Together, these analyses connect the evidence reported in prior studies to the contextual decisions involved in planning subjective evaluation.

We make the following contributions:

1. An evidence map connecting five dimensions of perceived effects, 35 observed subjective-indicator categories within a predefined taxonomy of 36 categories, and five tool categories across AI-Assisted Design Workflows, with explicit denominators for papers, evaluation cases, records, and tool instances.
2. A structured profile of reporting-support classifications across seven adapted evaluation standards, characterizing the distribution of recorded support and information gaps relevant to interpreting and reusing subjective evaluation evidence.
3. An analysis of contextual associations and predictive sensitivity that exposes the consequences of target-derived coding and demonstrates why stable descriptive distributions do not ensure stable recommendation rules.
4. A set of evidence-selection guidelines and a progressive-disclosure recommender specification that connect recommendations to traceable examples, contextual fit, and the limits of the available evidence.

## 2 Conceptual Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Perceived effects and subjective evaluation

Perceived effects identify the perspective from which the consequences of a system or intervention are understood. The term has been used in ACM research to describe users' interpretations and reactions to recommendation explanations [cite:okoso2025], the implications of research networking systems for collaboration [cite:schleyer2012], and collaborators' judgments of shared gaze during drawing [cite:ma2025]. These uses establish a family of situated research questions rather than a single standardized instrument or a universally agreed set of dimensions. Our opening definition draws together their common emphasis on human experience and judgment. It does not imply that every paper in the corpus uses the exact phrase perceived effects.

Subjective evaluation denotes the activity through which these judgments are elicited, interpreted, and used as evidence. Its evidence can be quantitative or qualitative. A numerical rating of workload, a comparison of two designs, and an interview about creative ownership provide different forms of subjective evidence. Their interpretation depends on the referent, the task, the evaluator, and the conditions of elicitation. Self-reports concern a person's own experience; expert ratings concern an evaluator's judgment of an object. Both can contribute to subjective evaluation, but an expert rating of a generated artifact does not by itself establish how the designer experienced working with AI.

We distinguish perceived-effect claims from the broader activities through which their evaluation becomes possible. Eliciting needs can establish whose values should define an outcome; constructing preference criteria can identify what should count as an improvement; and human labels can calibrate a model that subsequently supports design decisions. These activities are retained when they form part of the evaluation of perceived effects. Their purpose is coded explicitly so that upstream criteria or calibration judgments are not interpreted as demonstrations of downstream benefit. Similarly, a favorable score in a single condition is descriptive evidence unless the study design supports a comparison. This distinction concerns the strength and role of the inference, not the legitimacy of subjective evidence.

### 2.2 Evaluation in creativity support and AI-assisted design

Creativity support research has long faced the problem of evaluating open-ended activity whose valuable outcomes extend beyond task completion. Remy et al. review how creativity support tools are evaluated in HCI and identify diversity in the methods and criteria used [cite:remy2020]. Cox et al. extend this discussion by examining how evaluations characterize the broader impact of creativity support tools [cite:cox2025]. Ahsan et al. specifically review evaluative methods in human-AI co-creativity [cite:ahsan2026]. Together, these syntheses establish a foundation for examining how individual claims about perceived effects connect to indicators and tools, and what documented support makes those connections reusable.

The scope of the present review encompasses design outcomes, the design process, designers' cognition and affect, interaction with AI, and values situated in social or professional contexts. This scope includes creativity but extends to questions such as perceived workload, feasibility, trust, workflow continuity, and the fit of a design with user needs. It also separates evaluation practice from the capability of a particular AI technique. A technical improvement in generation quality may motivate a study, but it does not automatically establish an improvement in a participant's experience. Conversely, subjective evaluation may reveal a useful contribution even when a technical benchmark captures little of the activity that participants value.

This review therefore complements existing taxonomies by linking three levels of evidence and examining how those links are documented. The relationship is analytically important: two studies can assign the same indicator name to different questions, or use the same response format to measure different perceived effects. Retaining the original construct wording, instrument description, evaluator, and source location allows these distinctions to remain visible after category-level aggregation and supports the traceability of evidence used in subsequent evaluation planning.

### 2.3 Measurement foundations and evaluation evidence

Named instruments can help make subjective evaluation more explicit, but their use does not remove the need to justify the interpretation of scores. The Creativity Support Index provides one established approach to assessing support for creative activity [cite:cherry2014]. NASA-TLX addresses subjective workload through multiple components [cite:hart1988]. Lee et al.'s TOCHI review shows that implementation and reporting choices can substantially complicate the use and interpretation of an established instrument, and connects empirical analysis to practical guidance and a toolkit [cite:lee2026]. We adopt the general progression from evidence review to methodological analysis and guidance, while examining heterogeneous subjective evaluations rather than conducting a psychometric meta-analysis of a single instrument.

The CDC framework helps articulate the internal alignment of evaluation evidence [cite:kidder2024]. Our translation from concept, indicator, and measure to perceived effects, subjective indicators, and tools requires care: a tool is the means of obtaining a measure, and a scale name alone does not specify an item, response anchor, scoring rule, or interpretation. Accordingly, the extraction records these implementation details when available. Utilization-focused evaluation supplies a complementary account of why the same evidence can be adequate for one decision and inadequate for another [cite:patton2008]. Exploratory feedback, calibration, and comparative claims need different support because they serve different purposes.

The JCSEE Program Evaluation Standards provide a broader framework for examining the quality of evaluation practice [cite:yarbrough2011]. Seven standards inform the reporting dimensions examined here: relevance of information, validity, reliability, contextual description, design and analysis, explicit reasoning, and documentation. We adapt these dimensions to organize a structured assessment of the evaluation evidence available in published reports. The resulting classifications support comparison of reporting patterns and the definition of analytical samples; inclusion in a sample reflects its operational eligibility rules rather than certification of a study's overall quality.

The scope of this assessment is therefore the published account of subjective evaluation. A publication does not provide a complete record of research practice, and an unreported procedure cannot automatically be treated as an inappropriate procedure. Equally, mentioning an established instrument or analytical method does not by itself establish that the resulting interpretation is justified. Examining the correspondence among perceived effects, subjective indicators, tools, and context provides a way to make these evidential requirements explicit while preserving the limits of what published reports can establish.
