2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK

2.1 Perceived Effects and Subjective Evaluation

Perceived Effects concern how people experience, interpret, and judge the effects associated with a system, feature, or intervention. The term identifies an evidential perspective rather than a single psychological construct. For example, Okoso et al. use Perceived Effects to organize users' interpretations of and reactions to recommender explanations across several evaluative goals [cite:okoso2025]. Their account provides a concrete example beyond design, but does not establish a universal definition applicable to every system. Accordingly, Perceived Effects should be understood through the particular effects, people, tasks, and circumstances specified in an evaluation.

Subjective Evaluation provides access to these judgments through such methods as ratings, comparative choices, interviews, and reflective accounts. Its defining feature is the use of human judgment as evidence, rather than any particular response format. Numerical ratings can provide subjective evidence, while a human-recorded count can describe an objective event. Moreover, a judgment about an artifact's novelty, an account of workload during its production, and a belief that AI improved the result address different claims. Their shared human origin does not make them interchangeable. A review therefore needs to preserve what is being judged and how the judgment is elicited, alongside the identity and situation of the person making it.

This distinction is consistent with HCI accounts of user experience that extend evaluation beyond instrumental usability to affective, experiential, and contextual aspects of interaction [cite:hassenzahl2006]. Human-centred design similarly locates evaluation within an iterative process involving users and their contexts throughout the development of interactive systems [cite:iso2019]. These foundations motivate attention to Perceived Effects across design stages. They do not imply that every design activity is human-centred, or that every human response constitutes evidence of a system's effect. Rather, they establish why an evaluation must connect a situated judgment to an explicit design question.

2.2 AI-Assisted Design and Human–AI Co-Creativity

Creativity-support research has long examined how digital tools help people explore possibilities and develop creative work [cite:shneiderman2007]. AI-Assisted Design Workflows extend this agenda through systems that generate, transform, evaluate, or recommend design material. Such capabilities can alter both the artifacts produced and the distribution of decisions between people and systems. COFI provides a framework for describing interaction in human–AI co-creative systems, including the organization of contributions and communication [cite:rezwana2023]. A recent agency-focused review further organizes collaboration patterns and strategies across human–AI co-creation research [cite:zhang2025]. Together, these studies show why an evaluation's interaction setting and allocation of control deserve explicit description.

The consequences for Subjective Evaluation are substantive. A designer may evaluate the novelty of a generated alternative while also judging whether the system supports exploration, whether a recommendation is understandable, and whether the resulting work still reflects their intentions. These judgments refer to different aspects of the workflow and can diverge. Guidelines for human–AI interaction likewise emphasize managing expectations, communicating capabilities and limitations, and supporting correction and control as interaction unfolds [cite:amershi2019]. Such considerations make AI function relevant to the choice of Perceived Effects and to the conditions under which they are evaluated.

Empirical findings also caution against treating creative benefit as a unitary outcome. In a visual ideation experiment, Wadinambiarachchi et al. found that their AI-support condition increased fixation and reduced several aspects of divergent output [cite:wadinambiarachchi2024]. In short-story production, Doshi and Hauser found gains in individual creativity judgments alongside reduced diversity across outputs [cite:doshi2024]. These studies concern different tasks, interventions, and measures; they should not be collapsed into a general positive or negative verdict on AI. Instead, their differences illustrate the importance of specifying the criterion, evaluation object, and level of analysis. A review of Subjective Evaluation can reveal which claims are examined and how, without estimating a universal effect of AI on design.

2.3 From Perceived Effects to Evaluation Evidence

An evaluative concept does not determine its measurement automatically. Work on construct validity establishes the need to justify the relationship between a theoretical interpretation and the observations used to support it [cite:cronbach1955]. Contemporary measurement scholarship further emphasizes the transparency of construct definitions, instrument selection, adaptation, and scoring [cite:flake2020]. For a literature review, these concerns imply that a label such as usefulness or creativity is insufficient on its own: the reader also needs to know the specific indicator and the procedure through which evidence was obtained.

Established instruments illustrate the value of retaining these distinctions. The Creativity Support Index assesses a tool's support for creative work through six dimensions, rather than simply assigning a creativity score to its output [cite:cherry2014]. NASA-TLX addresses multiple sources of experienced workload [cite:hart1988]. Neither instrument is a generic substitute for every Perceived Effect. Their use requires an account of the evaluation target and the interpretation intended for the resulting scores. At the review level, the analytical object is therefore the connection between a Perceived Effect, its subjective indicator, and the tool used to elicit or organize evidence.

General evaluation frameworks provide a complementary account of that connection. The CDC Program Evaluation Framework aligns concepts with indicators and measures, situated within an evaluation question and design [cite:kidder2024]. Utilization-Focused Evaluation emphasizes the intended users and uses that should guide evaluative decisions [cite:patton2008]. These perspectives motivate attention to both the internal organization of evidence and the circumstances in which evidence will be used. In this review, we operationalize those circumstances through evaluation purpose, design domain, design stage, evaluation object, evaluator, target user, and AI function. This seven-part contextual model is an adaptation for the present research, not a taxonomy prescribed by either framework.

The Program Evaluation Standards offer a further basis for examining the adequacy of reported evidence [cite:yarbrough2011]. Our appraisal adapts selected standards relevant to the information available in research publications. This scope is consequential: a paper may provide insufficient detail for a reader to assess validity or trace an inference, even when the original investigators undertook additional work. Conversely, detailed reporting does not itself guarantee that an inference is sound. The resulting appraisal therefore concerns the evidence available to readers and the conclusions that evidence can support, rather than certifying the complete conduct of an evaluation.

2.4 Existing Syntheses and the Present Review

Several syntheses already address creativity-support research and its evaluation. Frich et al. map the landscape of creativity support tools in HCI [cite:frich2019]. Remy et al. examine evaluation practices and recommend stronger connections between evaluation goals, theory, domain expertise, and situated use [cite:remy2020]. Cox et al. review outcome measures in 173 creativity-support evaluations and identify opportunities to broaden evaluation beyond prevalent user-experience and output-quality measures [cite:cox2025]. More recently, Ahsan et al. systematically review how creativity is conceptualized and measured in human–AI co-creativity [cite:ahsan2026]. These studies provide substantial foundations for examining Subjective Evaluation and preclude positioning the present work as the first evaluation synthesis in this area.

The present review addresses a complementary unit of analysis: the linked evidence through which Perceived Effects are evaluated in AI-Assisted Design Workflows. It asks how Perceived Effects connect to subjective indicators and tools, how these choices are distributed across contexts, and how adequately the published evidence supports their interpretation. This focus includes creativity while also accommodating other Perceived Effects relevant to design work. It further allows evaluation contexts to be examined alongside evidence choices, rather than treating an instrument's overall frequency as sufficient grounds for recommending it.

Recent TOCHI work also demonstrates the value of translating an evaluation review into practical implementation support: Lee et al. connect a review of NASA-TLX use with analysis, guidelines, and a toolkit [cite:lee2026]. Our contribution follows this broader research-to-practice ambition with a distinct evidential basis. We use documented associations to support contextual retrieval and progressive disclosure of evaluation evidence. The resulting recommender is intended to make relevant precedents and their limitations inspectable. It does not establish a universally optimal measurement set, and its outputs must remain conditional on the coverage and quality of the underlying literature.
